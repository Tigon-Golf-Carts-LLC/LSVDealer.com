Low Speed Vehicles (LSVs): The Complete Guide

What they are, how they work, and where to buy one

Low speed vehicles are one of the fastest-growing categories of electric transportation in the United States. Compact, affordable, and street legal on neighborhood roads, low speed vehicles bridge the gap between a golf cart and a full-size car. This guide explains exactly what a low speed vehicle is, the federal standards that define the class, how fast they go, what it takes to drive one legally, and how to find low speed vehicles for sale near you.

What Is a Low Speed Vehicle?

A low speed vehicle (LSV) is a motor vehicle with four wheels whose speed attainable in one mile is more than 20 miles per hour but not more than 25 miles per hour on a paved level surface. A low speed vehicle is regulated under FMVSS No. 500 and must meet specific DOT safety standards to be street legal. Defined by the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration, a low speed vehicle must also have a gross vehicle weight rating (GVWR) of less than 3,000 pounds. Most low speed vehicles are battery-electric, though the federal definition is based on speed and weight rather than the type of powertrain.

How Fast Can a Low Speed Vehicle Go?

Speed is the defining trait of the class. A low speed vehicle tops out between 20 and 25 mph. That ceiling is intentional: it keeps LSVs light and simple while allowing them to legally share roads posted at 35 mph or less. If a vehicle can exceed 25 mph, it is no longer a low speed vehicle and must meet the far stricter safety standards that apply to conventional passenger cars.

Federal Safety Standards (FMVSS No. 500)

Unlike an ordinary golf cart, every low speed vehicle must be manufactured with the road-safety equipment required by FMVSS No. 500:

  • Headlamps, front and rear turn signal lamps
  • Tail lamps, stop (brake) lamps, and reflex reflectors
  • An exterior mirror on the driver's side plus an interior or passenger-side mirror
  • A parking brake
  • A windshield meeting the applicable standard
  • A Type 1 or Type 2 seat belt assembly at every seating position
  • A 17-character Vehicle Identification Number (VIN)

This equipment is what lets a low speed vehicle be titled and registered for road use, and it is the single biggest difference between an LSV and a standard golf cart.

Electric vs. Gas Low Speed Vehicles

The overwhelming majority of low speed vehicles on the road are electric. Electric LSVs are quiet, produce zero tailpipe emissions, and cost only pennies per mile to charge from a standard outlet. A smaller number of low speed vehicles use small gasoline engines, but electric drivetrains dominate the category because they pair perfectly with the short trips and low speeds LSVs are built for. Types of Low Speed Vehicles

  • Street-legal golf carts: Golf-cart platforms upgraded to full LSV specification for road use.
  • Neighborhood Electric Vehicles (NEVs): Purpose-built electric LSVs designed for community transportation.
  • Personal Transportation Vehicles (PTVs): Comfortable, car-like LSVs for daily driving.
  • Electric utility vehicles: Cargo-capable LSVs for work sites, campuses, and property maintenance.

Legal Requirements and Registration

Because low speed vehicles are federally recognized motor vehicles, most states require them to be titled, registered, and insured, and require the driver to hold a valid driver's license. Where you may operate a low speed vehicle — and whether local roads posted above 35 mph are off limits — varies by state and municipality. Always confirm the rules with your state DMV and local authorities before you buy. An authorized dealer can walk you through the exact requirements in your area.

Low Speed Vehicle vs. Golf Cart

The two are often confused. A golf cart is not street legal and is meant for private property or the course. A low speed vehicle meets FMVSS No. 500 and can be registered and driven on public roads. When a retailer advertises a "street-legal golf cart," they almost always mean a low speed vehicle.

Common Uses for Low Speed Vehicles

Low speed vehicles shine anywhere trips are short and speeds are low: planned communities and retirement villages, college and corporate campuses, resorts and hotels, parks and recreation areas, industrial facilities, and downtown municipal fleets. Their compact size, quiet operation, and low running costs make them a practical alternative to a second car.

Low Speed Vehicle FAQ

What is a low speed vehicle?

A low speed vehicle (LSV) is a federally defined class of four-wheeled motor vehicle with a top speed between 20 and 25 mph and a gross vehicle weight rating under 3,000 pounds. LSVs are built to Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard (FMVSS) No. 500, which requires road-safety equipment so the vehicle can be titled, registered, and driven legally on many public streets.

How fast can a low speed vehicle go?

By federal definition, a low speed vehicle has a top speed of more than 20 mph but not more than 25 mph. That speed cap is what allows LSVs to be street legal on roads posted at 35 mph or less in most states, while keeping them in a separate, lighter-regulated class from full-speed passenger cars.

Are low speed vehicles street legal?

Yes. When equipped to FMVSS No. 500 — headlights, turn signals, tail and brake lights, reflectors, mirrors, a windshield, a parking brake, seat belts, and a VIN — a low speed vehicle is street legal on roads posted at 35 mph or less in most states. Registration, insurance, and a valid driver's license are typically required; specific rules vary by state and locality.

What is the difference between an LSV and a golf cart?

A golf cart is designed for the course and is not street legal. A low speed vehicle meets federal safety standards and can be registered for road use. Many street-legal 'golf carts' sold today are actually LSVs — golf-cart platforms upgraded with the lights, mirrors, seat belts, and VIN required by FMVSS No. 500.
